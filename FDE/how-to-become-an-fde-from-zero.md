# How to Become an FDE From Zero: Capability Path & Experience Sharing

**Source:** [@Formulasearch (阿哲Phil)](https://x.com/Formulasearch/status/2084158215596486804)

## The previous article said half of FDE roles are fake — so how do you break into the real half?

In the last article, I went through FDE roles across four cities — Chengdu, Shanghai, Hangzhou, and Shenzhen — and concluded: **half the roles are doing real implementation, while the other half have merely renamed "on-site & outsourcing,"** and I gave three questions and three signals to tell them apart on the spot.

This article is about how to get in — what paths exist, what you need to know, how to prepare for interviews, and two similar things I'm working on myself. Finally, it answers the question the last article left open: **whether this role is worth entering at all.**

Let me start with a conclusion: **the entry barrier is far lower than most people imagine, but don't assume it's an easy, high-paying job.** Many people are kept outside the door not because they're unqualified, but because they measure themselves against senior-role requirements. Those who are already in the field aren't necessarily comfortable either, but overall it's a role that really trains you.

## 1. Three Entry Paths Into FDE

In recommended order: first see whether you can still take path A; whether or not you take A, path C is mandatory.

### Path A: Internship and New-Grad Entry — the Lowest Bar, and the Most Underrated

Across the four cities there are eight FDE internship roles in total, with daily pay of 120–350 RMB; big companies generally pay 250–350. The most relaxed education requirement says "no degree required," and some even say "high school." Shanghai also has a dedicated management-trainee track at 7K–15K.

One internship posting wrote its requirements quite specifically — they're almost the entry standard for this path:

> You've used a large model — not just to chat with it, but to **actually use it to solve some real problem**.

> You can at least write scripts in Python.

> You're not afraid to get your hands dirty; when you hit a problem you try solving it yourself first, rather than waiting for someone to tell you how.

The bonus points read: **you've done an AI-related project yourself — course assignments, personal projects, and hackathons all count.**

### Path C: Build Your First Real Delivery Yourself (🔺 the Main Line)

Whether or not you take the other two, this one is mandatory. Because interviewers ask what you've built, not what you've studied. No degree, certificate, or course can replace something that someone is actually using.

**Step one: complete a runnable, full project.**

1. The data is real;
2. AI is embedded in the workflow, not just used for chatting;
3. The result actually lands;
4. It's deployed on a server, not just running on your local machine;
5. **At least one person who isn't you is using it;**
6. **You can clearly say whether it works well or not.**

The last two are where most people fail to measure up.

**Step two: find your first real customer.** Its purpose isn't to make money, but to put you face-to-face with real uncertainty. Real customers describe requirements unclearly, change their minds midway, and provide messy data. This experience can't be obtained through self-study.

**Where to look:** acquaintances from your previous industry; small organizations without a tech team, such as law firms, clinics, design studios, and independent creators; people in your community who are currently complaining about some tedious task.

The first deal can be cheap or even free, but you **must clarify what to deliver and how acceptance works.** After it's done, you must write a retrospective.

**Step three: distill your method.** By the third customer you'll see things repeating; abstract them into templates. At this point you've separated yourself from people who just take orders — for them, the tenth engagement is as exhausting as the first.

**Step four: translate it into your resume.** Don't write "responsible for implementing and deploying the XX system"; instead, write it like this:

> The customer's original request was A; after on-site diagnosis, the real bottleneck turned out to be B. I designed solution C, used X weeks to first get one minimal end-to-end chain running for validation, and moved the metric from M to N. Along the way I distilled D, which was later reused across K projects.

**"Diagnosing the real problem, a minimal closed loop, measurable metrics, and reusability" — these four things are the entire difference between an FDE resume and an ordinary implementation resume.**

### Path B: The Policy Track — Taking Shanghai as an Example

Shanghai's human resources bureau and economic-information commission are running an FDE advanced training program with about 50 people in the first cohort; competition winners can be directly certified as engineers, and **Shanghai and Anhui have set up a joint certification committee** to promote mutual recognition. Shanghai Open University has launched the country's first dedicated FDE course, and Shanghai Institute of Technology has started a new Pre-FDE pilot class.

**The limitations are obvious:** higher time cost, geographically limited, limited slots, and having a qualification doesn't equal having a job. It's an add-on to path C, not a replacement for it.

## 2. There's Another Path: the AI Coding Direction

This one is mentioned separately because many people are already at the door without realizing it.

Some big-company FDE roles revolve entirely around "helping customer teams make good use of AI coding tools." There's also a 15K–18K role in Hangzhou whose requirements state directly:

> Be proficient in at least two AI coding tools, such as Codex, Cursor, Claude Code, Kiro, etc.

**If you're already using these tools to get things done, that is your qualification — you just haven't written it into your resume yet.**

This path's advantage is very concrete: you don't need to enter the client's intranet, you don't need to handle infrastructure-level engineering in the server room, and you use the very tools you already use daily. You're still doing FDE work — the scenario just shifts from the client's business to the client's R&D process.

**For people already using these tools to build projects, this is the highest-conversion entry point.**

## 3. Capability Requirements: Layered by Salary

You don't need to dig into the English terms in parentheses right now, but **you'll need them when searching for jobs and writing your resume** — that's exactly how job postings phrase them.

The two things not listed in the table but worth the most are: **measuring results**, and **asking the real questions**. Job postings keep repeating "effect evaluation" and "tracking business metrics"; the most direct phrase is "not satisfied with 'already delivered,' but pursue 'results meeting the standard'"; another requirement is "able to hear the pain points the customer hasn't articulated." Only by measuring results can you charge by outcome — otherwise you can only bill by hours. This is both a technical ability and a pricing ability.

The judgment of a team that has done this for three years: the scarcest capability isn't programming, but the ability to dive into an unfamiliar business and quickly figure it out — even a Gen-Z member of their team who majored in French can get up to speed. **Technology is the entry ticket; business understanding is the ceiling.**

## 4. What Interviews Ask, and What Recruiters Look At

In the interview records, the interviewer spent almost the entire hour pressing on project details, testing whether what you've built can hold up under scrutiny.

How to prepare: for every project you've done, question yourself five levels deep — why was it designed this way, what other options were there at the time, why weren't they chosen, what broke after launch, and how did you solve it. The places you can't answer are exactly where the interviewer will dig.

**What recruiters look at.** Reading the wording of a dozen-plus job postings in reverse, only three things keep recurring:

**Can you turn something vague into something clear; can you own it all the way through; can you explain things clearly to people who don't understand technology.**

They write the technical requirements out at length, but those are just screening criteria; the three things above are the real reasons for an offer.

**Prepare a ten-minute verbal presentation.** Take a scenario you're familiar with and practice until you can clearly explain who the customer is, what the real problem is, which segment you plan to unblock first, how you'll measure results, what the risks are, and how humans and AI divide the work. Being able to deliver this smoothly is more useful than memorizing ten technical terms.

## 5. The Business I'm Currently Exploring

I'm putting this here to make one point: **you don't need an offer to start being an FDE — what you do determines whether you count as one.** Combining my own dual background of designer + AI, I can find some relatively unique scenarios.

### 1. Providing technical support to architects and artists, including creation and website building

These clients almost never state specific technical terms — or, if they do, only someone who is also a practitioner can understand them.

An architect won't say "I want a responsive portfolio website"; they'll say: "I want to make a portfolio site? Something that lets me conveniently show my projects, works, and ideas to clients or peers, instead of sending a pile of drawings and public-account links."

An artist won't give you technical specs; they'll say "I want a clean atmosphere that expresses my reflections on nature and humanity — this isn't quite the texture I'm after."

The requirement in their head is a **feeling**, not a **spec**. My job is to translate feeling into spec.

The approach is exactly the same as described before: don't ask "what do you want," but "which part of what you see now is right, which is wrong, and where it falls short." Converge through samples, not through words. First make a tangible version for them to react to — that's more effective than three requirement meetings.

**The lesson this taught me: when a client can't describe their requirements clearly, it's not their problem — you just haven't found the right way to ask.**

### 2. Doing SEO content optimization for enterprises

I built my own workflow that produces content on schedule, reviews results regularly, and keeps optimizing.

This is already a complete FDE delivery: it fits into the client's existing rhythm, uses real data, first unblocks the minimal chain of "one piece of content from topic selection to publishing to data collection," **and the retrospective itself is the result measurement.** The workflow is an asset reusable for the next client, and with data to back it up, you don't have to negotiate by the hour.

**What's most valuable here isn't "I can do SEO," but turning something that depended on intuition into a workflow with metrics, that can be reviewed and handed off to someone else to run.**

**Next time you switch to a new client, after a bit of simple communication this entire process can start running again right away.**

## 6. So, Who Is FDE Not Suitable For?

Job ads won't mention any of this.

**First, business travel and on-site work are entry conditions, not bonuses.** The job postings literally include "no remote work," "accept overtime," and "accept travel and on-site work." If you can't accept this one, there's nothing more to discuss — after all, an FDE really does have to go out to the customer and battle in person.

**Second, this role isn't for people who want to build a career on technical depth.** You'll spend a long time adapting to other people's environments; three years later you may know five legacy systems across five industries, yet struggle to call yourself an expert in any single technical direction.

**Third, inside the client's company you may have no authority at all.** Everything is pushed forward through influence; people who can't accept this situation will find it very painful.

There are two more, rather subjective, ones — from a practitioner's own words:

1. If you think this job makes money quickly, I'd actually advise against entering — because then it's hard to understand human nature.
2. People who only want a stable job needn't force it either; FDE is ultimately a transitional role, and its end point is to eliminate itself.

### Self-Check Before Sending Your Resume

- [ ] Does my work include the "distilled and reusable" step?
- [ ] Can I name a result measured by outcome, not by hours?
- [ ] What have I actually accomplished with AI tools, or have I only used them for conversation?
- [ ] Is "can accept travel" something I genuinely accept, or only say I accept?

## 7. Is FDE Worth Entering?

### FDE Is Just an Intermediate Role That Will Likely Disappear — but Not Necessarily Because AI Fully Lands

I originally thought the answer was simple: once the product matures, the gap narrows and headcount shrinks. The very company that invented this role is an example — after its product matured, a large batch of FDEs converted to product engineering.

Later I saw a more realistic take: this model simply can't be pushed through in most Chinese companies, for three reasons, each more fundamental than the last.

**First, an FDE comes to transform processes, but in a company the only person who can change processes is the boss.** An outsider has no administrative power, no budget authority, and no HR authority; every step forward needs someone's signature. Those who won't sign aren't necessarily against you — they just don't want to vouch for your success, because if it fails the blame is theirs, and if it succeeds it won't necessarily count toward their own performance.

**Second, someone who understands business, technology, AND how to move an organization is a scarce commodity.** People who have both halves aren't willing to take implementation-salary for four jobs — pre-sales, delivery, training, and operations. The compromise usually means sending someone who only half-understands each side.

**Third, and most fundamental: what counts as success is never settled.** Is AI running = success? Is a business-metric improvement = success? Is reducing headcount = success? If this isn't fixed at project inception — and that consensus is extremely hard to reach, because each department wants different metrics — the FDE's work ultimately becomes something that can't be counted into any department's performance. A project no one claims is a project no one maintains, and three months later it's back to square one.

**So the temporariness of this role comes not just from products improving, but from projects dying.**

And there's an even faster change: today's hardest technical barriers are exactly the ones tools will dissolve first. Deployment, tuning, and environment adaptation can now filter out half the candidates, but they all have standard answers — and questions with standard answers are the first thing tools solve. What remains is understanding business and designing solutions, and that part has no standard answer.

### What Can You Actually Gain From Being an FDE?

One practitioner's phrasing, which I think is the most accurate thing said about FDE:

> What it shares with B2B sales is that **you run through the entire process**; what differs is that you get to be on the client side. If you can't understand what "being on the client side" means, then it's just outsourcing with a new shell.

The same person said **the biggest gain from FDE isn't money, but cognition, perspective, communication, judgment, and understanding of human nature** — before, a management trainee stayed in one position for half a year; now you can survey a company's business processes, profit model, and organizational structure in two months.

He also offered a concrete path forward: find a mid-tier company in some industry, build an efficiency-improvement solution, then take that semi-customized solution and win over similar companies one by one.

## Summary

The previous article's conclusion: half are temporary outsourcing, half are real AI implementation, and you have a way to tell them apart on the spot.

What this article adds is the second layer: **even if you identify it correctly and get in correctly, this job will very likely still disband. Whether you have something in hand when it disbands is the dividing line between outsourcing and real implementation.**

After outsourcing finishes, only hours remain — you leave empty-handed. Someone who has truly done it once takes away an understanding of an industry, a methodology of their own, and a group of people who have seen your delivery results. Both are roles that will disappear, but **one disappears and you reset to zero; the other disappears and you level up.**

One more thing worth thinking about: **the hotter FDE gets, the more it reveals how insufficient AI implementation capability is.**

All sides competing for talent, governments building channels, salaries multiplying year over year — these look like indicators of industry prosperity, but they're actually a list of debts. The model is strong, but it still can't read the screenshot inside a law firm's Word document, still can't handle that offline server in the client's machine room, and still can't judge which work should stay with humans. **Behind every FDE role is something AI should have done well itself but didn't.**

So it's more like a thermometer, measuring how far AI still is from reality. The day AI can cross that gap on its own, this role will quietly disappear — not as a failure, but as a success.

**It's very hot right now. Get in while the heat is high, learn the industry's current state and experience, and turn them into your own assets.**
