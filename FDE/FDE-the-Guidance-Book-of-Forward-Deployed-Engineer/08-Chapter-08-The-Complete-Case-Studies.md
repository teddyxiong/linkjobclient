# Chapter 8 · The Complete Case Studies

The methodology of the preceding seven chapters was all distilled from real field practice; here we restore it in full: five sets of cases, from the inventor of the model to its latest heirs, from Silicon Valley to China.

## 8.1 Palantir: Forging a "Crude Method" into a Moat over Twenty Years

### Founding: Building Software for Users You Cannot Ask

In 2003, Silicon Valley was still lying in the ruins of the dot-com bubble. Peter Thiel rallied a group of Stanford-bred young people to do something that sounded insane: build data-analysis software for U.S. intelligence agencies. The company took its name from the palantír—the seeing-stone in *The Lord of the Rings* that lets you see distant things.

The hardest part of this from day one was the user. Bob McGrew's now widely circulated recollection years later states the predicament most clearly: "One challenge of building software for spies is that I don't know any spies. You probably don't either. And even if you happen to find one and ask, 'So how do you actually work day to day?,' they usually won't tell you."

The entire methodology of the software industry—user interviews, surveys, usability testing—collectively failed in the face of this user group. Stephen Cohen, one of the founders, responded with an endearingly crude move: build a demo, show it to people at the intelligence agencies, and ask what they thought. The answer was merciless: "This is terrible—it has nothing to do with what we do." Cohen didn't retreat; he pressed: "So what would you want it to do differently?" Then he wrote down every piece of feedback, went back and revised it, and brought it back to their door when done. Round and round.

This loop is a genetic fragment of the FDE—it contains two intuitions that would later prove worth a fortune: customers in complex domains don't know what they want until they see something that works; and the fastest route to knowing "what they want" is to put the people who build the thing next to the people who use it.

### Taking Shape: From Stopgap to Organizational Strategy

Early Palantir moved along a "one customer, one custom build" path, and soon hit the problem every enterprise-software company hits: what the second customer wants is "subtly but critically different" from the first.

The standard fix is to extract commonalities, build a general-purpose product, and say no to differences. But Palantir's customers would not accept "no"—they were the CIA, they were U.S. troops on the battlefield. The person who truly pulled the company out of this dead end was an early employee, Shyam Sankar (later the company's president and chief technology officer). His solution was regarded as heresy at the time: build not two products, nor one rigid product, but a highly customizable platform, and dispatch engineers to embed at the customer's site to close the last mile.

Sankar's most critical move was a rebellion on the ledger. In the financial language of the software industry, "customizing for a single customer" is called cost of services—the enemy of margins, to be minimized. Sankar redefined it as "product discovery"—on-site customization isn't spending money, it's gathering intelligence for the product's next evolution. A single change of noun altered a company's capital-allocation logic: what others saw as a cost center became Palantir's R&D center.

The organizational form then took shape. Forward Deployed Engineers (internal codename "Delta") embedded with customers, "one customer, many capabilities mobilized"; deployment strategists (codename "Echo") were responsible for understanding the customer's mission and organization; platform engineers stayed in the rear, "one capability, serving many customers." Before 2016, Palantir had more Forward Deployed Engineers than platform engineers—at a software company, the majority of the troops were "at the front."

### Trial by Fire: Battlefields, Hurricanes, and Oil Fields

The value of the FDE model was repeatedly proven at one extreme site after another.

On the battlefields of Iraq and Afghanistan, roadside bombs were the number-one killer of patrols. The embedded Palantir engineers found that soldiers had zero interest in beautiful intelligence charts—all they wanted was "mark this road as suspicious on the map." The engineers threw together a crude mapping tool on the spot: a soldier taps, and the risky stretch becomes visible to the whole unit. This tool directly reduced casualties and was later distilled into a standard platform feature. You can't build this tool in a headquarters conference room. It can only appear in the moment an engineer and a soldier look together at the same highway.

The prototype site for this playbook came even earlier than the mapping tool. Around 2007, Sankar led a small team into the secure information room of the U.S. military's Joint Improvised Explosive Device Defeat Organization (JIEDDO) for two weeks of co-working. In the secure room even speakerphones were forbidden, so he tied a phone to his head with a rubber band to keep both hands free for coding—one ear listening to analysts' feedback, the other ear on colleagues at Silicon Valley HQ. Nineteen hours a day: demo, ingest data, collect feedback, fix on the spot.

After two weeks, the analysts said: this thing works. Sankar, however, was burned out. He called Karp: "This isn't sustainable, we're finished." Karp's answer later became company culture: turn this "unsustainability" into an institution. (Source: Appendix C)

At the 2012 Hurricane Sandy disaster-relief site, Palantir's forward deployed engineers stitched scattered rescue data into a real-time map to coordinate the deployment of relief resources. Oil drilling platforms, aircraft final-assembly halls, bank trading floors—the engineers took "on-site embedding" to a depth the software industry had never seen. Former Palantir engineer Barry recalls that the core components of the Foundry platform were born in Zurich, Houston, São Paulo, Toulouse, and Baku—a world map where every point was a site of on-site creation.

The cost was equally real. Barry's account is required reading as a cautionary tale for understanding this model: top engineers, global travel, lots of reinventing the wheel, free pilots—"for many projects the margin was literally negative infinity"; in 2014 the company's product strategy was, literally, "strong opinions, loosely held," chaotic enough to keep equity-holding employees up at night. What let Palantir swallow these costs were two things: the capital depth propped up by sky-high contract values, and a "run pilots like venture capital" mentality—most bets going to zero is fine, so long as the few winners win it all back.

Commercialization also died once before. The first enterprise product, Metropolis, flopped in the market; it was only on the second attempt, Foundry, that they got going. The turning point was Airbus: in the Toulouse factory, an A380 fuel-pump fault recurred repeatedly, and Airbus's own engineers had been stuck for two full years. Palantir's people came in, wired the sensor data into the platform, and cracked it in two weeks—the fuel sloshed away from the pump body when the plane climbed. A trivial fix preserved what was reported to be a multi-billion-dollar order.

Airbus's head of digital transformation, Marc Fontaine, later remarked publicly: "For the same problem, we used to take twenty-four months." (Source: Appendix C) Airbus became Palantir's most enthusiastic evangelist in Europe, building its own data platform, Skywise, entirely on top of it, connecting tens of thousands of aircraft and over 50,000 users.

This platform playbook was later proven once more at a life-and-death site. Tampa General Hospital in Florida had co-built a sepsis early-warning tool with a vendor; the tool was classified by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as a regulated medical device and forced out of service. For an ordinary customer, the story would end there; Tampa General's choice was to rebuild it themselves on Palantir's platform, led by Chief Digital and Innovation Officer Scott Arnold. By mid-2025, this in-house sepsis center had saved 569 lives, and the hospital stay for sepsis patients was shortened by 30%. (Source: Appendix C)

### Breakout: The Bootcamp Ignites a Commercial Empire

In 2023, generative AI exploded. Every enterprise wanted to "use AI," yet every enterprise was stuck at the same place: they didn't know how. Palantir's answer was to compress its two decades of on-site methodology into an industrial machine—the AIP Bootcamp.

The rules are minimal: customers come with real data and real problems; Palantir's forward deployed engineers sit with them and, in one to five days, build a deployable AI application prototype, free or for a token fee. Day 0 locks in an extremely focused core battlefield; Day 1 connects the data and builds the ontology (the semantic layer that models the objects and relationships of the customer's business); Days 2–3, engineers and the customer's technical staff write code back-to-back, wiring the LLM into the business workflow; Days 4–5, executives operate the system with their own hands and decide on the spot.

The conversion efficiency of this machine stunned the entire industry: starting from fewer than 100 pilots in 2022, the number of sessions doubled year after year, peaking in 2025 at nearly 6 per day; the 9–12 month sales cycle of enterprise software was compressed to weeks; pharmacy chain Walgreens deployed to 4,000 stores in eight months through the Bootcamp; market-research firm J.D. Power, once it learned the method, began running Bootcamps for its own customers—the model began to self-replicate. The contract cadence after Bootcamps was so fast that peers might not believe it: one large healthcare company, five weeks after attending a Bootcamp, signed a five-year agreement with $26 million in annual contract value (ACV); one global bank, after a one-month pilot, first signed $2 million, then four months later expanded to a three-year, $19 million ACV deal. (Source: Appendix C)

The financial results then exploded: U.S. commercial revenue in Q4 2025 grew 137% year over year; the "Rule of 40" (Rule of 40, the sum of revenue growth rate and adjusted operating margin) reached 127%, climbing to 145% in Q1 2026; single-quarter bookings hit $4.26 billion, with Net Revenue Retention of 139%; market cap at one point crossed $400 billion. The Wall Street analysts who had mocked its "human-wave tactic" began rewriting their research reports overnight.

The biggest vote of confidence came from the U.S. Navy. In December 2025, the Secretary of the Navy and Karp jointly announced the "Shipbuilding Operating System" contract: $448 million, first using Foundry and AIP to transform the submarine industrial base—two large shipyards, three naval shipyards, and one hundred suppliers. The pilot data was given earlier: scheduling went from 160 hours to 10 minutes, and materials review from weeks to one hour. The Secretary of the Navy specifically declared "this is not a concept, not a pilot, not research"—it skipped proof-of-concept and went straight to production, because the earlier embedded pilot had already proven the value. That same year, the U.S. Army also bundled 75 scattered service contracts into a single ten-year, $10 billion framework agreement for Palantir. The government's trust is compound interest accumulated over twenty years of being on-site. (Source: Appendix C)

An engineer who was embedded in Toulouse for a full year, Nabeel Qureshi, left an even more vivid footnote: four days a week he lived in the final-assembly workshop, working alongside manufacturing workers to write software for the A350 production line—he called it "the Asana for building airplanes" (editor's note: Asana, a popular Silicon Valley project-collaboration tool). A Silicon Valley engineer, living a full year in a foreign factory. (Source: Appendix C)

### Methodology Restored

Looking back at the first seven chapters, the Palantir case has a correspondence almost everywhere: the demo loop and "customers don't know what they want" map to Chapter 2, the strategic value of lighthouse customers to Chapter 3, the portfolio-investment logic of free pilots to Chapter 6, and on-site tools growing bottom-up into a platform to Chapter 7. The most fundamental creed runs through the whole book: complexity cannot be destroyed remotely, so someone must be present.

## 8.2 OpenAI: When the People Who Built ChatGPT Start Moving Bricks Themselves

### The Turning Point: From "The Model Is the Product" to "Deployment Is Strategy"

OpenAI's first seven years were a purely technical epic: the GPT series raced ahead, and ChatGPT set the fastest user-growth record in human history. In a company narrative like that, "enterprise delivery" sounds like somebody else's industry.

The turning point came in 2024. Enterprise-market data revealed something impossible to ignore: the now widely known "95% failure rate" from *The GenAI Divide* report was playing out; meanwhile the enterprise LLM market share map shifted dramatically—Menlo Ventures' industry tracking showed Anthropic climbing to about 32% in the enterprise market, while OpenAI fell from an early absolute lead of about 50% to about 25%. The gap in model capability was narrowing, and the standard by which enterprise customers voted with real money quietly became a different question: **"Who can actually help me get this thing working?"**

OpenAI's response came in two steps. Step one, in 2024 it formed a forward deployed engineering team—starting with 2 engineers, led by Colin Jarvis, rapidly expanding to a global network spanning San Francisco, New York, London, Dublin, Munich, Paris, Zurich, Tokyo, Singapore, and Sydney, growing from 2 to 52 people in a year. The job posting was unabashed: "Embed with customers where model performance is critical, delivery is urgent, and ambiguity is the default state," with up to 50% travel. Europe head Fournier put it more bluntly: "Market demand exceeded our expectations." (Source: Appendix C)

Step two, on May 11, 2026, it founded the "Deployment Company"—a joint venture controlled by OpenAI together with 19 top-tier capital partners (led by TPG, co-led by Advent, Bain Capital, and Brookfield, with Goldman Sachs, SoftBank, and Warburg Pincus participating, and Bain & Company, Capgemini, and McKinsey as founding partners), with initial investment exceeding $4 billion and a media-reported pre-money valuation of about $10 billion; simultaneously it acquired the applied-AI consulting firm Tomoro, with about 150 deployment engineers integrated wholesale. COO Brad Lightcap personally took command.

The structural design of this new company is worth putting in a business-school textbook, because it welds "where do customers come from" and "how does capital return" together. The investors are private-equity giants, and these giants hold hundreds of portfolio companies across healthcare, manufacturing, finance, retail, and logistics—they are the Deployment Company's ready-made customer pool; in exchange, OpenAI reportedly committed to these PE investors a 17.5% annual preferred return over five years (media reports; OpenAI has not confirmed), while retaining super-voting rights and control of strategic direction.

The acquired Tomoro is no nobody either: the engineers at this British applied-AI consultancy had previously done enterprise deployment at Tesco, Virgin Atlantic, and game company Supercell. In a single day, OpenAI assembled all three cards—"engineers, channels, capital." (Source: Appendix C)

A company valued in the hundreds of billions of dollars, holding humanity's strongest models, setting up a separate $10-billion company just to "send people to work at the customer's site"—this is the heaviest weight ever placed on the FDE model's scale.

### On-Site: John Deere's Farmland

The best showcase window for OpenAI's FDE methodology is its collaboration with John Deere. This nearly 200-year-old agriculture giant needed to solve a problem that grows from the soil: weed control. The traditional approach is to spray herbicide across the whole field—costly, damaging to crops, and environmentally expensive. The ideal of precision agriculture is "spray only when there's a weed," but the difficulty of identification lies in this: every field has different weeds, different crop growth, and different seasons.

OpenAI's forward deployed team's approach is a complete demonstration of the methodology in Chapters 2 and 4 of this book: fly to Iowa, go into the fields with agronomy experts, understand the precision-agronomy workflow and its constraints (including that non-negotiable hard deadline—the farming season); review hundreds of real field-operation cases with the experts, encoding "what counts as a good spraying recommendation" into a custom evaluation system; iterate rapidly on the model and system under the care of that evaluation system. Final results: chemical usage reduced by up to 70%, and farmer interaction frequency increased 6x. The pricing is also aligned with value: not a flat per-device fee, but a subscription billed by the actual acres where the technology is enabled—in 2024 alone, the system saved 8 million gallons of herbicide mixture, and by 2025 its coverage exceeded 5 million acres.

First, the hard deadline is the farming season, not the project schedule—the FDE's pace must obey the customer's business rhythm. Second, the evaluation system precedes model optimization—first define "good," then pursue "good." Third, the 70% figure was not a post-hoc marketing flourish; it was the target set jointly with experts before work even began.

### On-Site: BBVA's 120,000 People

Another benchmark is BBVA (Banco Bilbao Vizcaya Argentaria). The starting point was unremarkable—deploying the enterprise version of ChatGPT. But the compounding power of the FDE model lies here: enter with one tool, and in the end you transform the entire organization. Starting from 3,300 accounts, employees spontaneously created over 20,000 custom assistants (GPTs, proprietary AI apps employees can build themselves), 83% used it actively every week, saving about 3 hours per week on average; a year and a half later, the deployment expanded into a system covering 25 countries and 120,000 employees, and the bank's goal upgraded to "building an AI-native global bank." From "giving a tool" to "co-building an organizational form"—this is the ultimate form of "cultivating the installed base" from Chapter 6. (Source: Appendix C)

### Methodology Restored

The lesson of the OpenAI case lies in the "self-awareness of a model company": when model capabilities converge, deployment capability becomes the main battleground for differentiation; when the product form is undecided (no one knows what an agent should look like), the field becomes the only site of product discovery. Its three-stage workflow (co-create, validate, deliver), its engineering obsession with evaluation systems, and the capital-structure innovation of the Deployment Company (using PE capital's industry networks as customer channels) are all products of this self-awareness.

## 8.3 Anthropic, the Vertical Trio, and Platform Vendors: Four Variations on the FDE

If Palantir invented the model and OpenAI pushed it into the headlines, then Anthropic, a batch of vertical AI companies, and data-platform vendors show four variations on the FDE in different soils.

### Variation One: Anthropic—Its Safety DNA and the "Auditable" Differentiation

Anthropic's FDE sits under the Applied AI team, and its job posting has two telling qualifiers: one is "safety and reliability"—all delivery must meet Anthropic's safety standards; the other is "defend the company's mission on the front line"—the FDE is also an ambassador for its values. In 2025, this team expanded fivefold in a year; lead Kate de Jong explained: "The needs of a Fortune 500 bank and an AI-native startup are completely two different species." (Source: Appendix C)

The direct product of this differentiation is that "auditability" became a product feature. Its collaboration with fintech giant FIS is the benchmark: Anthropic's forward deployed engineers embedded with FIS to co-design a financial-crime-detection agent, compressing anti-money-laundering investigations from hours to minutes, with every decision fully replayable and traceable, first landing at Bank of Montreal and Amalgamated Bank. In a heavily regulated industry, every AI decision must be explainable to regulators; if you can't explain it, you can't enter—Anthropic turned this admission line into a selling point.

Evidence that "safety paranoia" became an enterprise-market killer app is written in customer reviews. Cybersecurity firm Palo Alto Networks deployed Claude to 2,500 developers; the engineering director's verdict was direct: "They care more about safety than anyone else—every meeting is about safety impact. As the largest cybersecurity company, that matters to us." Result: feature-development speed up 20–30%, junior developers completing integration tasks 70% faster. Pharma giant Novo Nordisk uses it to write clinical-research documents: reports that once took a dozen-plus weeks now come out in ten minutes (vendor's official case figure). Ride-hailing platform Lyft wired Claude into customer service, cutting average resolution time by 87%; customer-service software company Intercom's support agent Fin, after switching to Claude, lifted resolution rate from 23% to 51%, and up to 86% after deep customization—the full process of both these deployments, from pilot to company-wide rollout, is written in Chapter 4. Meanwhile, it partnered with consulting giant Accenture to train 30,000 Claude specialist consultants, and its enterprise customer count grew from under 1,000 to over 300,000 in two years. (Source: Appendix C)

A deeper difference lies in the official commitment to "knowledge transfer": the explicit goal of embedding is to let FIS "independently build and scale its own agents in the future." Writing "teach the customer" into the contract weakens your own irreplaceability in the short term, but in the long term it is the strongest rebuttal to the "vendor lock-in" doubt (being chained to a single supplier, hard to migrate)—Gartner analyst Coquelle predicts that before 2028, 70% of enterprises will abandon such solutions due to supplier cost and skill hollowing-out (media paraphrase; see Section 5.1). Anthropic's approach defuses this time bomb in advance.

In May 2026, Anthropic was reported to be forming an enterprise-AI-services joint venture with Blackstone and others, reportedly around $1.5 billion, focused on embedding Claude into mid-market operations—taking the field the same day as OpenAI's Deployment Company. Two model giants completing the institutionalization of "deployment is strategy" on the same day says more about the FDE's coordinates than any analysis.

### Variation Two: Sierra and Decagon—The Startup's "Delivery Is the Product"

Sierra (founded in 2023 by former Salesforce co-CEO Bret Taylor and former Google VP Clay Bavor; by its October 2024 funding round its valuation had already reached $4.5 billion, and reportedly kept climbing afterward) turned the FDE model into a business model itself: customers don't pay for software, they pay for "resolved conversations"; implementation is managed by Sierra's forward deployed team, and the customer only contributes brand voice and operating rules. Result: a gathering of brand clients like Sonos, Casper, WeightWatchers, and ADT; the fastest publicized time-to-launch was four weeks, with annual contracts starting at about $150,000 (third-party estimate; see Section 6.3). Paying by outcome naturally anchors Sierra's pricing to customer value—the most aggressive real-world specimen of "pay for results" from Chapter 6.

Worth noting is what Sierra internally calls this role: not FDE, but "Agent Engineer." Its lead, Natalie Muller (five years ex-Palantir, ran law-enforcement, defense, and infrastructure customers), explains that the name should describe "the shape of the technical work," not just "an obsession with the customer"—building voice agents requires a special kind of "taste" (what sounds human, what sounds right), a craft narrower and deeper than a general FDE. The collaboration with ticketing platform Vivid Seats is her team's brightest battle report: launched in four weeks, resolution rate up 40%, customer satisfaction up 35%—and the mechanism by which conversation data feeds back into the product means "ten thousand people asking about the same feature in a month" can immediately jump the priority queue. (Source: Appendix C)

Decagon (one founder, Ashwin Srinivas, ex-Palantir; total funding $231 million) took another path: productizing delivery experience directly into agentic operation flows—letting the customer's operations staff define multi-step workflows in natural language, which the AI executes deterministically. Client list: Notion, Duolingo, Eventbrite, Rippling. Reportedly, Chime reached 70% resolution, Duolingo's issue-routing rate hit 80%, and ClassPass cut support cost by 95%. Decagon's FDE more often plays the role of "the person who teaches the customer's team to use the system"—the goal of delivery is to give the customer's non-technical staff self-service capability, the best practice of "can it be used by people who can't code" from the "four questions of productization" in Chapter 7. (Source: Appendix C)

### Variation Three: Harvey—Breaking Into Places Ordinary Software Can't Reach

The legal industry is the "Bermuda Triangle" of enterprise software: top law firms enforce strict confidentiality discipline, data doesn't leave the domain (data residency, data doesn't leave the environment the customer specifies), and partners each run their own fief—self-service SaaS finds few wins here. Harvey (founded 2022; after a new funding round in March 2026 its valuation had reached $11 billion) muscled its way in using the FDE model.

The company's origins are a bit unusual: of its two founders, one is a litigator from a law firm, the other a scientist from DeepMind (Google's AI lab); the $5 million seed round came from the OpenAI Startup Fund. (Source: Appendix C)

Reviewing its playbook, almost every step maps to the methodology in this book. Its first major client was locked in as global top firm Linklaters (February 2023, 3,500 lawyers, 43 offices), followed by PwC (over 4,000 legal professionals), Cleary Gottlieb, and McFarland. Inside Linklaters, the liaison David Wakeling (head of market innovation) became the in-firm axis of the deployment; the rhythm was to start from a single practice group, push through a partner champion, and only expand horizontally after six months of real combat. Customer research was the hardest part: partners pay but don't do the work, associates do the work but don't pay, and knowledge-management lawyers see the blind spots on both sides—so the FDE's interview system must penetrate all three layers at once; per Harvey's deployment-side review, the hardest part of deployment isn't the model, it's the customer-research loop. The cycle also ran long: six to nine months per firm, with the FDE responsible all the way from the document-management system's data pipeline to the per-partner adoption demo.

The effect is a rare, explosive growth trajectory in vertical AI: over 100,000 lawyer users and 1,300 organizations, covering most of the top 100 U.S. law firms; annual recurring revenue (ARR) grew from about $100 million in August 2025 to about $190 million in January 2026; valuation jumped four times in a year—$3 billion, $5 billion, $8 billion, $11 billion. (Source: Appendix C)

Harvey proves where the boundary of the FDE model lies: in any market where the three mountains—confidentiality regimes, data residency, and key-user autonomy—exist simultaneously, productized delivery is impossible; only a person going in can bring the system in. And such markets—law, healthcare, finance, government—happen to be the highest-margin enterprise-service markets on earth.

### Variation Four: Databricks and Snowflake—The Platform Vendor's Embedded Delivery

Variations don't only happen at model companies. The customers that data-platform vendors face are mostly not AI-native companies, but traditional industries buried in mountains of data they don't know how to use—and here too, delivery can only be done by engineers embedding in.

First American is a long-established company in the U.S. title-insurance industry, maintaining images of over 8 billion publicly recorded documents, a large batch of which are historical scans with no metadata at all. It needed to classify page by page and extract data fields page by page from about 4 million title policies tracing back to the 1930s, but commercial LLMs fell short on accuracy. Databricks engineers embedded directly into the customer's team—Prabhu Narsina, the customer's VP of data and AI, recalls: "They met with us almost daily, wrote code with us, debugged with us." The eventual route was to fine-tune an open-source model on the platform, training on about 50 GPUs, compressing the fine-tuning cycle from over two months on other platforms to two weeks. Project bottom line: cost down 70%, schedule shortened 75%, data-extraction volume up 10x. (Source: Appendix C)

The collaboration between managed-service provider Ensono and Snowflake turned "firefighting" into "fire prevention": since its prediction engine went live it has analyzed over 75 million events and 9 million alerts, warning before a ticket turns into a service incident—major incidents down over 20%, fault-repair time compressed by up to 70%. (Source: Appendix C)

These two cases fill in an easily overlooked piece of the variation genealogy: FDE is not the patent of model companies. Any company that has a platform and is willing to send engineers into the customer's site can enter this arena—the only difference is whether the field experience eventually settles into platform capability, or dissipates into yet another piece of human-delivery work.

## 8.4 China Cases: Planting FDE in the Soil of "Project-Based" Work

China is the world's most peculiar enterprise-software market: it has the most hardworking delivery engineers, the most demanding customization requirements, and the deepest "project-based curse"—big enterprises demand customization, vendors lose money on every deal, costs can't be amortized, they degenerate into client-side outsourcing, and specialization hollows out. The fate of the FDE model on this soil is a question that must be answered separately: is it the antidote to the curse, or a new skin for the curse?

How twisted this situation is comes through most clearly in the sighs of three practitioners. The first sigh comes from database company DolphinDB, in a long industry essay asking "Who Is Destroying China's Enterprise-Software Industry," whose answer is six mountains: free-riding, open source, outsourcing, bidding, "digital-tech subsidiaries" (the in-house digital-tech subsidiaries that large conglomerates set up), and a distorted market structure—China's top engineering talent is long consumed in non-standard delivery of "heavy customization, relationship-driven, price-war" work. The second sigh comes from a veteran of enterprise software on the *Hardcore Hacker* podcast: "Customization is the natural enemy of SaaS; this curse can only dissolve naturally when the market matures." The third sigh comes from Buqiong, former president of DingTalk: many companies get stuck at one or two hundred million yuan in revenue—hard to acquire customers, hard to deliver, hard to operate, high customization cost, and economies of scale are simply out of the question.

36Kr summed up these three sighs: "Productizing software amortizes the R&D cost of project-based work; SaaS amortizes the sales cost—only project-based companies suffer the most, because project costs expire the moment you leave and can't be spread out." (Source: Appendix C) These three sighs are the China question the FDE model must answer.

### The Big-Tech Path: Volcano Engine and Huawei

Volcano Engine, under ByteDance, is the Chinese internet giant that has most explicitly raised the FDE banner: in 2026 it set up a dedicated FDE team to deeply co-create with industries and benchmark customers. Tan Dai, president of Volcano Engine, in a public interview, echoed this book's definition almost word for word: "FDE is not sales, nor pre-sales; they must have strong technical-delivery capability, especially the ability to land AI code." He also revealed that team members are deliberately configured with diverse industry backgrounds (for example, sending someone with a bioengineering background to engage the biomedicine industry), and it currently covers key sectors such as automotive, healthcare, education, finance, and semiconductors. In July 2026, Volcano Engine also signed a strategic partnership with consulting giant EY, with the two sides planning to build a thousand-person-scale FDE team, "exploring a new model of full-stack AI delivery." (Source: Appendix C)

The weight of the big tech players is clearest in the heaviest industries. In February 2025, Shanghai's Ruijin Hospital released the clinical-grade pathology large model RuiPath, jointly built with Huawei: 16 GPUs, two months, to complete training on millions of pathology slides, cutting the data-preparation cycle by 80%. Pathology is one of China's most resource-scarce medical fields—the doctor gap is huge, and the primary-care concordance rate (the agreement between primary-care and top-hospital final diagnoses) is low—this model covers the cancer types afflicting about 90% of China's annual new cancer cases. Dean Ning Guang's statement at the launch can be read as the premise of such joint deployments: "We only use technology that can withstand verification." (Source: Appendix C)

The innate advantage of big tech doing FDE is a thick platform base (Volcano Ark provides an integrated base of models, toolchains, and cloud infrastructure); the innate challenge is the big tech's sales-system inertia and the financial inertia of "delivery is a cost center"—whether the FDE team can attain Palantir-style organizational status of "the field is R&D" determines whether it is a real FDE or a pre-sales support department wearing a new name.

### The Pioneer Path: Writing "FDE Is Not On-Site Outsourcing" Into the Homepage

More noteworthy are a batch of local startups. Without a big-tech base, they use the clearest language to distinguish FDE from China's old species. Qimeng Technology, a service provider focused on real-estate and facility-management, wrote on its website what is currently China's most refined definitional distinction of FDE:

> First, FDE delivers by stage and accepts by results, while on-site outsourcing settles by hours worked; second, FDE brings a product base to do the engineering, while on-site outsourcing writes and patches from scratch on the spot; third, FDE leaves when done, with capability deposited in the system and your team, while on-site outsourcing stays longer and longer, and when the person leaves the system stops.

These three sentences correspond to three core propositions of this book: pay for results (Chapter 6), platform base (Chapter 7), and knowledge transfer (Chapter 5). It also built a "turn-away mechanism"—it won't take scenarios that aren't validated, data that can be exported from a single spreadsheet, or clients who only want to learn about AI—"we'd rather you start later than have you start at the wrong moment." This is the conscious practice in the China market of "refusing the expensive proof-of-concept graveyard" from Chapter 2.

Another service provider, Qiantuo Technology, moved the FDE model into the compliance-most-sensitive financial industry, and distilled a clear three-stage methodology: scenario diagnosis of one to two weeks (identify the three to five highest-value core scenarios, quantify ROI) → embedded delivery of eight to sixteen weeks (full-stack delivery from model selection to production integration) → production deployment and continuous evolution (used daily by business units, iterated continuously with the business), while emphasizing "data residency"—this is a local adaptation to the regulatory environment of Chinese financial institutions. (Source: Appendix C)

In June 2026, a practitioner at a domestic investment institution posted a long essay online with a striking title: "FDE, Silicon Valley's Hottest Job This Year—We've Been Quietly Doing It for Three Years." The essay records their real experience using this playbook in Chinese enterprise services: forward deployed engineers "dive into one customer after signing, with unbounded capability, truly install AI into his business"; the measure is "not how much code was written or how many documents delivered, but whether the system is actually being used and whether the client's business has improved"; and that summary identical to this book—"going live isn't the end; he has to stay until the system becomes the client's daily routine and the client's own people can take over." This essay was widely shared in domestic practitioner circles, because it was the first to clearly lay out, in Chinese, a comparison table: product engineers are responsible for the roadmap, pre-sales for the signature, implementation for the acceptance sheet, customer success for the renewal—and FDE, for whether "the client's transformation and per-capita productivity (output per person) truly rose." (Source: Appendix C)

### Control Group: The Set-Top Box and the Paper Contract

On who FDE should and shouldn't be deployed with, Wu Guangyu, CEO of Baidao Data, gave two sharply contrasting customers at an entrepreneur roundtable.

One is a set-top-box client with an extremely clear need—"how to use AI inside a set-top box." FDE soaked with them and ultimately built a companion agent that watches shows with you and chats about gossip, with a very high ROI. The other is an overseas-expansion client whose boss opened by wanting to invest $1 million in AI, yet every time they signed a contract he printed it out and circled edits by hand, not even using Word's track-changes. Wu Guangyu's judgment: "A client with no digital foundation like this will surely produce nothing if you deploy FDE into them. We told him directly: first buy 100 Gemini accounts and get everyone using them, then talk once you have a concrete plan."

His conclusion matches the rejection mechanism of Chapter 2: the premise of FDE is that the client has a clear point of need. Don't lightly deploy into vague, boundary-less needs; you can split them into phase one, two, three, do the clear part first, and after delivering results the follow-on needs will naturally convert into a long-term service contract. (Source: Appendix C)

### The Other Side: The Vacuum Layer and the Dirty Work

But China's FDE practice isn't all like the above. Word from the front line is much rougher.

*Growth Hacker AI Weekly* recorded the observations of front-line executor Shen Yue: in many domestic projects, FDE is more like vendor outsourced labor, doing the dirty, thankless grunt work of filling holes and cleaning up messes. In a ten-million-level state-owned-enterprise project she experienced firsthand, the big boss signed the contract and then went silent; no one in the project was truly responsible, and the FDE was stranded in a vacuum layer—no decision-maker above, no executor below—and in the end could only study "reporting logic" to fight for acceptance. Another, a private Guangdong home-furnishing company, where the factory-second-generation boss came straight to the point: help her cut 6,000 people down to 3,000. But what AI can replace is tasks, not positions, and the project struggled forward amid repeated scoldings.

Shen Yue's summary: the difficulty of China's FDE lies in the layering of the demand side, in weak enterprise digital foundations, and in bosses lacking patience—and the over-promises front-end sales make to sell product all end up being paid back by FDE. She also has a line I quoted as the epigraph of Chapter 4: "No matter how technology evolves, the human nature and power-responsibility games inside an organization remain a harder problem than technology." (Source: Appendix C)

### Another Voice: Is FDE a Good Business?

Doubts don't only come from the execution layer. At the same roundtable, Wu Xiankun, founder of Kuse.ai, raised questions about the FDE model itself: "FDE is hard to be a good business model." His reasoning is direct: do it shallow and the model vendors will do it themselves; do it deep and it's essentially still outsourcing, with high delivery cost and hard to scale. "Serving state-owned enterprises, you can't refuse needs outside the project scope—where is your leverage? Will you, in the dead of night, doubt you've become cheap outsourcing—this is a problem everyone doing FDE must face."

His solution is rather radical: AI Roll-up. Since clients worry about data leaks and teams resent outsourcing as betraying the original intent, just buy the company—everyone becomes a shareholder, interests align, and the whole company is turned upside down and remade; after the remake, use the same methodology to keep acquiring the next one. His logic: if AI transformation truly has that much value, then you should own the business, not sell hours. (Source: Appendix C)

This doubt may not have an answer, but it resonates strangely with the Gartner prediction cited in Chapter 5—that before 2028, 70% of enterprises may abandon FDE-led solutions: for the FDE model, the hardest gate to pass is its own cost structure. This book doesn't intend to give a standard answer, but it is a question everyone wanting to enter should think through first.

### Three Special Propositions for China's FDE

Synthesizing these practices and industry observations, for FDE to hold in China it must answer three questions American peers need not answer.

First, pricing. The U.S. market can accept subscription, usage-based billing, even outcome sharing; but the muscle memory of Chinese clients is "buyout plus project-based acceptance." A viable transitional form is the "hybrid model." Pay by stage upon acceptance, suiting Chinese clients' procurement habits; write value metrics into the acceptance terms, injecting the outcome gene into the transaction; then sign an annual operations-and-evolution contract to gradually cultivate renewal awareness. Copying outcome-based pricing wholesale will, in most industries, die in the procurement department.

Second, platform base. The economics of FDE depend on "platform base plus on-site customization," yet the problem of many Chinese software companies is precisely "field presence, no platform"—every deal is a from-scratch, human-powered coding business. For these companies, more important than copying FDE's form is shoring up FDE's foundation: first deposit the highest-frequency customizations as reusable components, then talk about forward deployment. Without the replication leverage of Chapter 7, FDE in China will only degenerate into "on-site outsourcing with a nice name"—which is exactly why the local pioneers wrote "brings a product base" into the definition.

Third, talent. Chinese engineering culture—willing to endure hardship, fast to respond, full-stack across the board, accustomed to solving everything on the customer's site—is precisely the closest fit to FDE's requirements; that is the "opportunity." But the "constraint" is that for the past twenty years this talent has been trapped in a man-day pricing (charging by engineer headcount × days) outsourcing system, and market price signals have long undervalued them. The spread of the FDE concept in China may bring a profound side effect: re-pricing China's hundreds of thousands of delivery engineers. When they learn that their peers across the ocean earn a median total compensation of $385,000, this industry's value coordinates will loosen.

Evidence of price loosening was already visible on job boards in 2026. On the demand side, the recruitment market opened a pricing tier: FDE roles at top big-tech and AI companies post monthly salaries roughly in the RMB 20,000–80,000 range, with senior total packages starting at RMB 400,000—a multiple-times gap versus traditional implementation engineers (see Section 1.8 for specific market rates and tiers). On the supply side, the government is moving to supply talent: after Shanghai opened the first FDE-themed training class at the end of 2025, in February 2026 it launched a transformation cohort for industry engineers, focused on three industries—smart manufacturing, finance, and healthcare—with over 200 signing up for the first cohort, three cohorts planned for the full year, and it has been included in the municipal professional-technical-talent knowledge-update program. (Source: Appendix C)

There is also a more fundamental anchor, hidden in the financial reports of traditional software. In the 2025 annual reports, Yonyou's revenue per employee was about RMB 480,000 and Kingdee's about RMB 620,000; that same year, Palantir's revenue per employee was about $1 million—a tenfold-plus gap in per-capita productivity. This is both what the "project-based curse" looks like financially, and the biggest imagination space for the FDE model in China: whoever can pull delivery engineers' per-capita output from "man-day" pricing to "result" pricing simultaneously rewrites the market price of these hundreds of thousands of people and the financial structure of this business. (Source: Appendix C)

Established enterprise-software companies also confirm this judgment on the talent side. Yuan Xin, president of SAP Greater China, on the *LateTalk* podcast, said that the roles of forward deployed engineer and traditional ERP (enterprise resource planning) consultant are merging, and what's most lacking right now is "the composite talent who has both product-engineering capability and deep business understanding." She also warned: the biggest challenge in AI landing is organizational inertia, not technology—the historical debt of basic data architecture won't disappear on its own just because AI arrived. (Source: Appendix C)

### Judgment

FDE won't cure all the chronic ills of Chinese enterprise software, but it offers a rare chance at "rehabilitation": redefining "being close to the customer" from a marker of low-end outsourcing into a marker of high-end capability. China has no shortage of engineers willing to go to the field; what it lacks are three other things: the platform, methodology, and pricing structure that make field work compound. Whoever fills these three gaps first has a chance to break out in this largest and most complex enterprise-service market on earth.

## 8.5 An AI Startup Team's 180 Days: A Full FDE Field Retrospective

The final case comes from a Chinese AI startup team I tracked and researched (anonymized at the team's request, here called "Company N"). It has no Palantir platform, no OpenAI halo, but its 180-day journey completely demonstrates how this book's methodology operates on a resource-limited team. To protect commercial information, the numbers have been obfuscated, but the proportional relationships and decision logic remain real. Precisely for this reason, this case reads differently from the previous groups: the facts above can be checked one by one against Appendix C; please read this group as a complete "process demonstration"—its value is to string the whole book's playbook together in chronological order; for factual judgments, defer to the earlier cases.

### Days 1–30: Choose the Battlefield, Not Grab the Contract

Company N does enterprise-knowledge-base AI, and had three potential customer leads: a top-tier securities firm (big budget, grand demands), a regional chain-retail group (medium budget, specific pain points), and a top-tier hospital (big influence, sensitive data). The sales instinct is to pounce on the securities firm. But following the method of Chapters 2 and 3, the team ran a triple check and on-site due diligence: the securities firm's problem was a "cosmic-scale demand" (the first meeting already wanted to cover the whole company), and there was no clear business owner; the hospital's pain was real, but the data-residency constraint made the delivery cycle uncontrollable; the retail group's CFO personally watched the project, the pain was specific ("the operations-supervision reports for 3,000 stores—regional managers simply can't keep up"), and although the data foundation was messy, it was reachable.

The team chose the retail group—its lighthouse value wasn't the highest, but it passed all three checks. This is the discipline of Section 3.8: schedule by strategic value and learning value, not by contract size.

The passed-over securities firm deserves a footnote. "Refusing" isn't the only answer to this question. For a large client with very high lighthouse value but fuzzy needs, there is a third play: don't do delivery, first sell a paid diagnosis—two to three weeks, fixed fee, producing a scenario-priority report and a data-readiness checklist, effectively turning that "graceful off-ramp" from Section 2.3 into a formal product. If the other side truly has transformation resolve, the diagnosis process will boil the grand demand down into one clear first scenario, and signing an MVD follows naturally; if the other side just wants to write "AI" in its annual report, the diagnosis fee is the litmus test—a client unwilling to pay even for a diagnosis shouldn't have been worked with anyway. Company N didn't take this path at the time. Retrospecting with this book's framework, this was probably the only "correct but regrettable" decision in the 180 days: the refusal preserved capacity, but it also handed a potential top-tier lighthouse to a competitor willing to sell a diagnosis.

### Days 31–75: On-Site, the Shadowing Method, and a "Killed Big Plan"

Two engineers and one industry consultant went on-site. For the first two weeks they wrote not a single line of product code; what they did was the shadowing method: following regional managers on store visits, watching how they chase store data in WeChat groups and make weekly reports in spreadsheets. The key finding came from a "workaround": the regional managers simply didn't look at the reports in the company's data system; the data source they truly trusted was a table the store managers hand-filled every day—because "the system's data is three days late and often wrong."

This finding overturned the initial big plan (an intelligent data-analysis platform), and the team narrowed the minimum viable deployment to a small incision: **build only a "store anomaly daily report"**—every morning at 8 a.m., aggregate the previous day's anomaly signals from 3,000 stores (sales fluctuations, inventory anomalies, complaint spikes) into a daily report readable in three minutes, pushed to the regional managers' WeChat. Real data, a single pain point, a six-week hard deadline.

### Days 76–120: The Hidden War of Activation

Going live is only the beginning. The activation phase hit two textbook obstacles. One was trust calibration: in the first week, the AI misjudged two stores' promotional peaks as "abnormal spikes," and managers grumbled in the group. The team didn't argue; within 48 hours it wired the "promotion calendar" into the judgment logic, and—the key move—publicly thanked the manager who reported the error, in the group. The other was influencer cultivation: a senior regional director (the uncrowned-king type) initially watched coldly; the team invited him to participate in revising the evaluation criteria, and his three pieces of experience were written into the system rules. Two weeks later, he became the system's most tireless evangelist.

By Day 90, the daily report's organic open rate had stabilized above 85%. By Day 120, the retail group's CIO voluntarily demonstrated the system at the quarterly business meeting—the internal champion had been armed as a salesman.

### Days 121–180: The First Brick of Replication

The renewal negotiation was a foregone conclusion, but the team made two more important moves. First, it deposited the assets reused repeatedly in this delivery: the retail-industry data-ingestion component, the "anomaly signal" evaluation framework, and the store-scenario due-diligence checklist—the first scenario playbook took shape. Second, using the retail group's case (the client agreed to co-publish), it opened the door to a second client—the channel-management department of a fast-moving-consumer-goods brand, with an isomorphic scenario. The second project's delivery cycle was 40% shorter than the first: the replication flywheel turned its first loop.

180 days, a small team, no funding news, no disruptive technology. But it validated the book's most plain conclusion: this methodology asks for no platform and no halo—only that every step be honestly completed.
