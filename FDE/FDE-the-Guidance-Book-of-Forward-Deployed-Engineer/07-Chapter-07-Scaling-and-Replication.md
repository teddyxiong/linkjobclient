# Chapter 7 · Scaling and Replication

> "If you don't turn what you learn in the field into product assets, all you get is a pile of custom projects."
> — Barry, former Palantir Forward Deployed Engineer

## 7.1 Using Replicability to Leverage Delivery

The ideal state of internet growth is to have one user bring in more users — the growth engine shifts from externally purchased to self-generated. The question the FDE must answer is the same: every time you finish a customer, the next one should be easier to do — not by adding headcount, but by banking the experience of the last engagement.

This proposition is the book's "last mile," because it is the final line of defense between the FDE model and the consulting and outsourcing industries. Take scalability out of the playbook described earlier — what is the outcome? An elite team that can fight the hard battles takes on a customer, delivers to that customer, earns enough to cover costs, then takes on the next one — revenue growth is perfectly linear with headcount growth. Congratulations, you have reinvented the consulting firm.

Barry put this dividing line most bluntly in his memoir: "If you don't turn what you learn in the field into product assets, all you get is a pile of custom projects." McGrew frames it from another angle: the hallmark that the FDE model actually works is that "the amount of customization per subsequent customer decreases" — if the custom work you do does not decrease as the customer count grows, you are not doing FDE, you are running a headcount-times-days (people × days) billing outsourcing business.

The leverage of replicability comes in three tiers — each harder than the last, and each more valuable than the last.

- **Tier 1 leverage: Knowledge replication (the playbook).** Write down "how to do it" as documentation — playbook templates, checklists, training materials. It shifts dependence on individual experience into dependence on organizational memory: the ramp-up time for new hires drops from a year to three months. This is the most basic leverage, and most teams stop here.

- **Tier 2 leverage: Component replication (tools and code).** Turn "what was done" into "what can be used directly" — integration connectors, evaluation frameworks, deployment templates, industry data models. The starting point for the next team is no longer zero, but the shoulders of the last project. The automation assets described in Section 4.7 belong to this tier.

- **Tier 3 leverage: Product replication (platform capability).** Abstract "recurring customization" into a standard platform capability — from then on, this need no longer requires any engineer to be on-site. Palantir's ontology (the semantic layer that lets models operate on enterprise data), Decagon's Agent Operating Procedures (AOP), and Sierra's agent platform are all products of Tier 3 leverage. This tier changes the cost structure of the entire business: the first two tiers lower delivery cost, the third tier eliminates delivery cost.

The progression through these three tiers is the full version of McGrew's "gravel road and highway" line: the FDE builds the gravel road (solves the immediate problem), the component team lays the gravel base (so the next vehicle rolls easier), the platform team pours the highway (from then on, everyone can travel).

## 7.2 Bad News Travels Far — Spreading the Postmortem of Failed Deployments

Turning accidents into assets is a scarce organizational capability. An FDE organization's attitude toward failure determines whether it can possess the ability to replicate: failure is the raw material of replication, and an organization that covers up failure is burying a gold mine as trash.

Let's be clear about one thing first: failure in the FDE model is not an accident, it is inevitable. Barry's recollection is merciless: "There were plenty of failures — piles and piles of time, money, and travel expenses, burned spectacularly." Palantir went so far as to write acceptance of failure into its system: treat a pilot as if it were venture capital — most will die, and once dead, they must be postmortemed. Barry specifically reminds us: to those projects that crashed, those teams that spun their wheels, those engineers who burned out, you should also say thank you — they paid for the lessons with their own money. This is the hardest lesson to learn, because the engineer's nature is to celebrate success and hide failure.

Turning failure into organizational assets requires three mechanisms.

The first is the decriminalization of postmortems: the only rule of a postmortem meeting is to address the issue, not the person. The moment a postmortem becomes tied to performance, people begin to embellish their failures — and an embellished failure has zero teaching value. Decriminalization is not about abandoning accountability, but redefining "accountability" as "whether you honestly and completely extracted the lesson." The contributor of an honestly postmortemed failed project may give more to the organization than one that succeeded by luck.

The second is the structuring of lessons: the output of a postmortem cannot be a "be more careful next time" sentiment; it must be retrievable, actionable knowledge — what are the high-risk signals for this customer type? Which integration assumption got overturned? Which evaluation metric was designed wrong?

The answers to these questions should enter the "minefield" chapter of the playbook and the due-diligence checklist for new projects, so that the next team's on-site diligence automatically carries historical lessons. Only when a failure's lessons enter the process will the next team actually use them.

The third mechanism is the most counterintuitive: the appropriate external spreading of failure — share failure lessons externally after desensitization. As mentioned in Section 3.5, Barry's "airing our dirty laundry" article became the best sermon for the Palantir model. That article contained no success gospel; it was full of piles of burned time, money, and travel expenses, and one crashed project after another; it was precisely this style of writing that let readers recognize at a glance that this came from someone out of the trenches — enterprise clients have heard too many booth pitches, they can tell what is a pitch and what is the truth. A team daring to publicly dissect its failures sends the signal "we have already paid tuition for this industry's wrong turns" — exactly what enterprise clients most want to hear.

With the three mechanisms covered, let's walk through a complete autopsy of a failure. The components of the script below all come from the real cases earlier in this book; the assembly is a common way this industry dies.

A manufacturing company's AI project died in its ninth month. In month 1, the project was already sick at inception: the group issued a mandate to do AI, the IT department led it, and no business unit signed on — the "no man's land" signal of Section 2.3, which the vendor saw but pretended not to, because the contract value was tempting. Months 2 through 4, the validation period produced beautiful metrics on sample data — the client's security review refused to release real data, "look but don't touch," and the second high-risk signal lit up too; the project manager chose to build the thing first and deal with it later. Month 5, the demo was a huge success and acceptance passed. From month 6, real data came in, and the model began giving self-consistent but wrong answers against coding rules from three years ago; the business unit had never claimed ownership of this project, and now no one was willing to clean up. Months 7 through 8, usage quietly declined, and someone began asking in meetings "is this thing actually worth it." Month 9, budget season, the contract was uprooted — no incident, no argument, just no one spoke up for it.

The postmortem of such a project usually writes "the client organization was immature" in the cause-of-death column. That is an embellished failure. An honest postmortem per the Section 2.3 checklist: of three high-risk signals, two lit up, and every one was visible before signing. The real lesson is just one sentence: this project's date of death was set in month 1; the following eight months were just going through the motions. Only when the autopsy reaches this depth will "business owner sign-off" and "real data in place" move from suggestions to hard gates on the next due-diligence checklist.

## 7.3 Ride the Momentum: Standing on the Industry's Tailwind

Using the attention of external events for your own benefit is a classic move in marketing. Teams in the FDE lane are standing on an epic tailwind, and the window of that tailwind will not stay open forever.

The industry tailwind of 2025 to 2026 was formed by the convergence of several air currents: 1) *The GenAI Divide* report made the "95% failure rate" a worry for every CIO — the client's worry is your opportunity; 2) OpenAI founded a deployment company, and Anthropic was reported to be forming a similar joint venture, the two pieces of news breaking on the same day and pushing "deployment" onto the financial headlines — the money the giants spend to educate the market benefits the whole industry; 3) FDE roles saw 729% annual hiring growth, making this role a regular in tech media — even "should we hire an FDE" became a kind of corporate anxiety. These three air currents together created a rare situation: clients are actively coming to you to discuss what you want to sell.

The first layer of action for riding the momentum is "defining the agenda." The attention of a tailwind is a public resource; whoever's content defines the agenda harvests the attention. The VC firm a16z (Andreessen Horowitz) set the tone for the entire lane with one forceful industry essay; communities like OpenFDE aggregate the right to define even as they aggregate practitioners. For a specific company, the best way to seize the right to define the agenda is to make public what you have been keeping in the vault: playbooks, failure postmortems, value data — exclusive first-hand insight is the only currency of agenda-defining power.

The second layer of action is "binding to the industry agenda." The biggest driver of enterprise AI procurement is upgrading from "efficiency" to "survival" — CEOs are pressed in the boardroom with "what is our AI strategy," and that anxiety transmits into budget. Translate your solution into "boardroom language": not "we can help you optimize your customer-service process," but "we can let you produce a quantifiable AI report card at the next board meeting." The same delivery, anchored at a different agenda level, reaches a different budget pool.

The third layer of action is "build assets during the tailwind, don't consume the tailwind." The tailwind will stop. The discussion the report stirred up will cool, the media's buzzwords will go stale.

Therefore, the most precious thing during the tailwind is not signing a few more deals, but converting the attention the tailwind brings into long-term assets: lighthouse cases, the authority of industry methodology, a replicable delivery system. When the tailwind stops, only the team holding cases, methodology, and a delivery system will stand firm.

## 7.4 Building the Cross-Customer Propagation Loop

Word-of-mouth propagation that doesn't rely on advertising happens mainly through three circles in the FDE business.

Circle one: the practitioner community. The FDE is a professional community still in formation — in 2025 it was scattered inside various companies; by 2026, there were dedicated communities like OpenFDE, dedicated compensation reports, dedicated interview guides. The formation of a professional community is a godsend dividend for first movers: contribute content to the community (methodology, tools, data), and your name becomes bound to this emerging profession. The path of concept propagation has always been this way: the pioneering evangelists ultimately become synonyms for the concept.

Circle two: the client's industry community. Every industry has its own small circles — bankers' forums, law-firm-partner annual meetings, hospital-administrator summits. The trust dividend described in Section 3.3 flows mainly in these closed circles.

The ticket into these circles is not advertising, but an invited-speaking client advocate — your job is to give him something worth presenting, and well presented. The ten minutes a client shares on your behalf at his industry summit beats a hundred days at your booth at a trade show.

Circle three: the ecosystem-partner network. Section 3.7 covered the customer-acquisition value of ecosystem bundling; here we emphasize its propagation attribute: the solution architects at cloud vendors, the consultants at consulting firms, the project managers at system integrators — these people flow through different client sites every day — the "reliable team" list in their mouths is the fastest and most accurate promotional channel. The key to working this circle is to let them "win": let ecosystem partners gain performance, cases, and reputation from working with you. Those who help others succeed get remembered by the whole network.

Ecosystem bundling in 2026 also grew a deeper form: AI companies embedded their forward-deployed teams directly inside partners. The partnership between coding-agent company Cognition (maker of Devin) and global IT services giant Cognizant is the template — an embedded forward-deployed engineer team stationed inside the partner, responsible for project screening, engineer mentoring, and outcome measurement, borrowing Cognizant's decades-accumulated client relationships to scale its product into large enterprises in healthcare, finance, and insurance. When Databricks reorganized its entire professional services into forward-deployed teams, it likewise listed its global network of hundreds of partners as one of the four elements of scaling. At this stage, what the partner network propagates is no longer just word of mouth, but your people stationed in their offices.

The flywheel (self-reinforcing positive loop) formed by the three circles combined is: delivery creates stories, stories propagate through the circles, propagation brings new opportunities, new opportunities create new stories. The difference between this flywheel and traditional marketing is that its fuel is entirely "value already delivered" — the essence of word-of-mouth propagation is to make every past effort make future customer acquisition easier.

## 7.5 The Built-in Viral Factors of the Product

Good products spread themselves — the line "sent from so-and-so mail" in an email signature is the earliest self-propagation design. The systems an FDE delivers can also embed "built-in diffusion mechanisms," letting the system itself become a salesperson for expansion.

Factor one: visibility design. The outputs of a system will naturally be circulated — weekly reports, analysis results, approval flows. Let every output carry its "provenance": a "Generated by System X" in the report footer, an action-entry link inside an anomaly alert.

When a beautiful analysis report is forwarded to the director of the next department, whoever sees it will naturally ask "what made this?" The essence of visibility design is to let the system's value exhibit itself in the daily workflow, rather than relying on dedicated reporting.

Factor two: collaborative access. Design features that require cross-role collaboration: the case annotated by an investigator needs review by a supervisor, the analyst's report needs confirmation by the business side — every collaborative action naturally introduces the system to a new user. The new users brought by collaboration are not "promoted," they are "brought by the work itself," and their acceptance is entirely different.

Factor three: a self-service exploration layer. Beyond the core delivery, leave a low-risk practice area (sandbox): client employees can try "let AI do me an analysis" on their own. The usages that grow in the sandbox are often the most vital expansion leads — a scenario an employee in some department plays with himself has deeper roots than a scenario a salesperson pushes ten times. Let the whole company help you discover, while the FDE team focuses on the hard problems.

Two new products in 2026 turned this factor into an open card. Sierra launched "Ghostwriter" in March: clients upload their own SOPs, historical conversation records, even a photo of a whiteboard, or describe a goal in plain language, and the system automatically generates a production-ready agent covering three channels — voice, conversation, email — in over thirty languages. For the first time, the client's business people could "build agents themselves," while the forward-deployed team shifted from "build each one by hand" to "acceptance and backstop." Decagon's Agent Operating Procedures walked this path even earlier: a client's customer-service supervisor writes the process in natural language ("for returns over 30 days, first check membership tier; if it fits policy, process it") without waiting for an engineer's schedule — reportedly, Chime reached a 70% resolution rate, Duolingo's issue-routing rate hit 80%, and fitness platform ClassPass cut customer-service cost by 95%. (Source: Appendix C) When clients start building themselves, your role shifts from construction crew to design institute.

Factor four: cross-boundary value evidence. Each quarter, automatically generate a "this quarter's value report" and let it flow naturally to the client's executive layer. This report is both the renewal engineering of Chapter 5 and a stepping stone for vertical expansion — every number the executive sees plants a seed for "where else is this system worth using."

Company-wide promotion itself is also an engineering effort, and its capacity bottleneck also needs to be solved. Booking.com is the sample: rolling out its enterprise search platform to 14,000 employees was the first company-wide AI platform adopted in the company's history; the training and promotional materials supporting this rollout also sped up — the production cycle of a single promo video was compressed to a quarter of the original. The value the system delivered turned around and became the system's own promotion capacity.

The common principle of the built-in factors is: to get others to spread for you, you must first let them genuinely realize value. Any design made purely for propagation will be identified in the enterprise scenario as "the vendor's little trick" and met with resentment. The enterprise market has no viral spread, only a "chain reaction of word of mouth" — slow, but every step is solid.

## 7.6 Navigating Organizational Politics in Scaling

On the road to scaling, what stops you is usually people's turf; technology is actually the easy part to solve.

When a system expands from one department to ten, you are in effect rewriting the map of power inside the organization: information monopolies are broken (data a department alone held is now visible company-wide), approval authority is compressed (things the AI answers in seconds no longer need three days of layered signatures), professional barriers are leveled (a senior employee's "secret sauce" is encoded into the system). Every one of these changes creates silent enemies.

To handle organizational politics, FDE veterans have distilled three pieces of wisdom.

- **Wisdom one: expansion must bring along the "local."** Every time you enter a new department, first find the "local tyrant" in that department — the most prestigious senior employee — and invite him to participate in the adaptation process: "For this scenario, what's different about your department?" His answer makes the solution more accurate; his participation makes the solution flow unimpeded in his turf. People won't oppose what they helped build — this is one of the few reliable levers in organizational behavior.

Harvey's expansion in top law firms is a classic textbook for reading organizational politics. Inside a firm there are simultaneously three layers of people: partners — the ones who pay, but don't do the work themselves; associates — the ones who do the work, but don't sign; knowledge-management lawyers — belonging to neither side, yet able to see the blind spots of both. A generic survey would compress the three layers into the same set of dropdown options, yielding a bunch of distorted averages; Harvey's forward-deployed team instead designed separate interviews and demos for the three layers: show partners "how much money this deal makes," show associates "three fewer late nights this week," show knowledge-management lawyers "the firm's tacit knowledge finally has a place to settle." A deployment must make all three layers feel like beneficiaries at once — miss any one layer, and the system gets quietly shelved at the corresponding step. (Source: Appendix C)

- **Wisdom two: make every step of expansion have a "beneficiary."** Before expanding, calculate clearly what each related department gets: the department whose data silo was pierced gains globally-visible analytical capability; the department whose approval authority was compressed gains focus on exceptions (AI handles the routine, humans focus on the difficult). Before expanding, think through and articulate clearly what each related department can get — expansion that can't figure this out looks, in the other party's eyes, like a land grab.

- **Wisdom three: always leave "dignity" for the old order.** The process replaced by the system was once designed by some capable person; the experience encoded was once some expert's life's work. In the rollout messaging, respect for the old order is respect for its defenders: "This process supported the company for the past ten years; now we're amplifying its ten years of experience a hundredfold." Whoever leaves enough dignity for the old order will face half the covert resistance when the new system goes live.

All three wisdoms are about the client's organization. There is another layer of politics on the road to scaling, hidden inside your own company: the rights and responsibilities of sales versus delivery.

The root of this layer of politics is misaligned incentives: the sales bonus hangs on bookings, the delivery reputation hangs on results — the impulse to sign and the discipline to deliver are naturally at odds. Handle it with three written tables.

- **Division-of-labor table:** Sales is responsible for "whether to sign," delivery is responsible for "whether it can be done"; the veto power over custom requirements belongs to delivery, but a veto must come with an alternative — if you only say no and give no path, delivery becomes, in sales' eyes, "the person blocking business."
- **Split-the-money table:** First-order commission goes to sales; the bonus for renewals and expansions must be shared with the delivery team — as calculated in Section 5.1, the cost of renewal approaches zero, and all the value comes from delivery quality; if renewal bonus goes entirely to sales, the company is telling everyone with its wallet that "signing beats doing well."
- **Take-the-blame table:** Delivery incidents are assigned by "who made the promise" — incidents caused by sales over-promising go into sales' assessment; pits delivery dug itself go on delivery's head.

None of the three tables need to be complex, but they must be written down; unwritten rights and responsibilities will detonate all at once in the year with the most orders and the largest team.

## 7.7 Using the Playbook to Bank Replication Efficiency

Within the Tier 1 leverage, the engineering detail of the playbook is the easiest to underestimate — for most teams, the day they finish the playbook is the day it gets the most reads.

- **The playbook's first attribute: it lives in the process, not lies in the knowledge base.** A playbook that can only be searched in a document system might as well not exist. The effective approach is to embed it in the workflow: during a new project's due-diligence phase, the system automatically pops up the due-diligence checklist for that customer type; during the deployment phase, the checklist is a hard gate in the task system; during the postmortem phase, updating the playbook is a required output of the postmortem. Knowledge not embedded in process is just archeological material. At the end of this road, the process begins to execute the playbook itself: Ramp's engineering team let an AI assistant first do multiple rounds of clarification with the account manager, then draft the requirements document — by its disclosure, saving about 20% of the requirements-definition time.

- **The playbook's second attribute: organize by "scenario," not by "function."** Failed playbooks are written by company org structure ("delivery process," "technical spec"), while successful playbooks are written by client scenario ("financial anti-money-laundering scenario playbook," "manufacturing production-scheduling scenario playbook," "law-firm knowledge-base scenario playbook"). Each scenario playbook contains a fixed seven-piece set: one, typical pain points and fit-test essentials; two, the due-diligence checklist (the high-risk signals peculiar to this scenario); three, known minefields of data and integration; four, the metric templates for evaluation and acceptance; five, the role map for change management; six, the reference structure for pricing and scaling; seven, the lesson library from historical postmortems. The reason Harvey's playbook in law-firm scenarios could replicate to PwC and Cravath was that it turned the experience of the first client (Linklaters) into an inheritable scenario asset.

- **The playbook's third attribute: it must be "alive" — with an owner, a version, and depreciation.** Each scenario playbook appoints an owner (usually the most senior delivery team), forces a review-and-update after every project, and does an overall overhaul every six months — deleting the outdated, merging the duplicates. A playbook without an owner will, a year later, have a new hire follow it straight into a pit that was already fixed — which is worse than having no playbook.

The relationship between the playbook and Tier 2 leverage (component library) and Tier 3 leverage (platform) is: the playbook governs judgment (what to do in what situation), the component library governs craft (tools you can use directly), the platform governs capability (functions that need no one on-site). With all three in place, a newly formed FDE squad can reach 80% of an old team's combat power within two weeks — this is the real meaning of replication.

All three of these solve the replication of "things." But the scarcest asset in the FDE model is the person who can go on-site, and such people barely exist in the hiring market — Vinoo Ganesh, who designed Palantir's rotation training program, put it bluntly: "You can't build a real FDE organization through hiring; you can only grow it." After all, traditional engineering roles deliberately separate engineers from the client's reality; on-site judgment can't grow in an office.

"Growing" doesn't mean not picking seedlings. Palantir's signature interview step is to throw the candidate a deliberately ambiguous real problem — for example, "design a system that routes a city's emergency calls to the right responder" — and have him decompose it on the spot, writing almost no code; by candidates' general feedback, the most common mistake is preparing for this interview as a pure software-engineer interview. What it screens for is the person who can first pull structure out of a fog.

The method of growing is rotation: send engineers in batches into real customer deployments; over time, more than 250 engineers went on-site through this path, and alumni are now scattered across OpenAI, xAI, Anduril, and a host of other companies. The most critical improvement to this training program was aligning the rotation role with the product the engineer would go back to build — be a user first, then a builder. For companies without the conditions for rotation, he left a six-month self-service cultivation checklist:

1. The first two weeks, sit in on customer calls; what you record is not feature requests, but "how does this person spend their day";
2. The second month, end-to-end own one customer incident;
3. The third month, go on-site, find a small pain point fixable within a day, and fix it that week;
4. The last few months, maintain a weekly-updated "What I Learned From Customers."

This checklist is itself a playbook — a playbook for cultivating people.

The starting point of "growing" is "hiring" — where the seedlings come from, first have a map. The three pools with the highest conversion rate in practitioners' experience: 1) solutions architects at big tech — understand technology, have seen clients, but lack experience being accountable for outcomes; 2) technical consultants at consulting firms — understand business, can present, but lack hands-on work; 3) star users on the client side — understand pain points best, and hiring them makes them natural candidates for "echo." Conversely, use pure back-end R&D with caution — not a capability issue, but they are too far from the client's reality, and on-site judgment has to grow from zero.

The last sieve before bringing them in is the "problem decomposition" interview from Section 1.7. The same question — "a bank's compliance team manually checks 30,000 transaction alerts a day, 90% false alarms, what do you do" — the gap between good and bad answers looks like this. The dangerous answer rushes straight to technology: "Put a classification model on it." Three sentences reveal the bottom: he didn't ask how alerts are generated, who bears the cost of misjudgment, what the compliance officer trusts. The good answer starts with questions: who set the rule for judging false alarms? Who is responsible if one real alert slips through? Which step in the existing process is slowest? Then gives the minimal cut — "Don't make the judgment first; just pre-sort, pushing the most likely false alarms to the end, so humans check the high-risk ones first"; finally volunteers the trade-off — "false positives remain, but manpower is halved, and every suggestion is traceable."

## 7.8 Planning and Polishing the Productization

The crowning achievement of Tier 3 leverage is crystallizing on-site experience into product.

First, the most spectacular success case: Palantir's Foundry. Today this platform with annual revenue in the billions of dollars, underpinning the company's hundreds of billions in market cap, had its core components born on client sites all over the world — Zurich, Houston, São Paulo, Toulouse, Baku, and beyond — tools that local forward-deployed engineers each built to solve local problems,after years of natural selection and platform consolidation, ultimately grew into a unified product. Barry recalled this process as "completely bottom-up": in 2014 the company's product strategy was literally "strong opinions, loosely held," letting the field bloom freely and absorbing what survived into the core.

This process is no pastoral idyll; it has a strict internal logic, refinable into the "four questions of productization."

- **Question one:** Is this on-site solution "idiosyncratic" or "common"? Test: has the same class of problem appeared independently at three or more clients? The same pain point at three clients is a product signal, while one client's special requirement may just be a quirk. Palantir's rule of thumb is to let "multiple forward-deployed teams reinventing the wheel" naturally expose the commonality — when three teams each build something similar, the platform team knows: the time to consolidate has come.

- **Question two:** Is the cost of generalization less than the benefit? McGrew warned: on-site code is "fast but crude," and the cost of generalizing it is "often higher than writing the first version." The productization decision must account for this: generalization investment versus the custom-cost saved across future clients. If it doesn't pencil out, leave it at the component layer; don't force it onto the platform — every capability on the platform is a lifelong maintenance commitment.

- **Question three:** Can it be used by "people who can't write code"? The users of on-site tools are engineers; the users of platform capabilities are everyone. The hardest step in productization is often not technical but interaction: turning an engineer's script into a function a business person can configure. Decagon's Agent Operating Procedures (letting operations define processes in natural language) is the template for this step — once you pick the right abstraction layer, the user population can expand a hundredfold.

- **Question four:** After consolidation, is there still room on-site? This is the most subtle balance: consolidate too much onto the platform, and the on-site team degrades into configurators, and the soul of the FDE model (on-site creativity) dies; consolidate too little, and the leverage of scaling never lifts off. Palantir's solution is to maintain "aggressive authorization of the front line by the base" — leadership only sets goals; the playbook belongs to the field. The platform makes the common things easy, so the on-site team has surplus capacity to chew on the hard bones that were previously undoable.

With the four questions answered and the platform built, the final discipline just appears: the order of expanding openness. Latin American e-commerce giant Mercado Libre made it a textbook. This company opened its internal platform to 17,000 developers for self-service: any team assembles AI applications from nodes and skills, with built-in testing, but never sees source code — cognitive load is pressed to the minimum, and the safe bet is entirely on the quality of the platform's guardrails.

The pace of expanding openness is so slow it borders on conservative: first use AI on tasks with low cost of error — cataloging, translation, review summarization — cataloging alone increased the processable merchandise volume a hundredfold within two years; only after low-risk tasks accumulated enough data and organizational trust did the platform touch money-involving customer-dispute mediation, and even then it first opened about 10% at a single major site, with humans kept in the copilot and escalation channels. Its COO put it plainly: cataloging and summarization are "fun and useful, but not transformative"; the real transformation is letting the system autonomously handle complex, high-risk work. It's just that such a system has to first be fed by years of low-risk tasks.

Those who doubt this flywheel get their most forceful rebuttal always from gross margin (the share left after subtracting direct costs from revenue): send people on-site, how could gross margin be high? History has given three answers. ServiceNow's gross margin at IPO was 63.2%, criticized as "too much like a services company" — ten years later, 79%, with a market cap near $200 billion; Workday's at IPO was 54.1% — now 76%; Palantir's at IPO was 79%, and by 2025 had reached 82%, prettier than the vast majority of "pure software" companies.

The VC firm Insight Partners summarized it incisively: these companies that went heavy on implementation and services during the platform-switching period are precisely the ones that held on when everyone was running the other way. The secret is that gross margin can climb year by year — as the platform sediment thickens, clients can do more of the implementation work themselves (the Bootcamp model lets a large amount of integration work be done on the client side), and service cost is continuously diluted — the opening never looks good, but it runs lighter and lighter as it goes. (Source: Appendix C)

The negative specimen is equally ready at hand. Bessemer Venture Partners dissected India's IT services industry: industry leader Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) doubled its revenue over the past decade, and its headcount nearly doubled too, yet revenue per employee stayed stuck at about $49,000 — growth still depends entirely on piling on people. A services company that doesn't complete the platform-switching transition will, a decade later, just be a bigger services company — the industry-level version of "reinventing the consulting firm."

Beyond gross margin, there is a more intuitive yardstick: revenue per employee. Put three side by side — TCS still at about $49,000; Accenture's fiscal 2025 about $89,000 ($69.7 billion revenue, 779,000 employees); Palantir's 2025 about $1 million ($4.48 billion revenue, 4,429 employees). From outsourcing to consulting to FDE, per-capita output differs by an order of magnitude.

On the startup side, the multiple is even more direct: Harvey's about $190 million in annual recurring revenue supports a $11 billion valuation, about 58x — the market is clearly pricing it as software, not services. Whether this pricing is right will only be known five years from now, but at least it shows: capital markets are willing to pay a software multiple for a delivery model whose "per-capita output approaches software's." This comparison is also the hardest answer to "is FDE just consulting in a new skin": whether it is or not is not judged by the title, but by which direction per-capita output and gross margin are climbing. (Source: Appendix C)

Pull the lens back from the giants to the ground, and run the rough numbers on a 10-person squad — this is the book's own estimate; every assumption is laid out, and you can plug in your own numbers and recalculate. Cost side: by the caliber calculated in Section 5.3, a fully-loaded FDE in the US market approaches $500,000 a year in cost, so 10 people is $5 million; in the China market, converted by the compensation levels of Chapter 1, the cost side is several million to over ten million RMB. Capacity side: as calculated in Section 3.8, a small squad can concurrently deliver at most two high-quality projects; 10 people split into three squads, rotated over a year, deeply serves about 10 to 15 clients.

Revenue side: a deep-service project at $100,000 to $300,000 per contract, 10 to 15 clients a year, yields $1 million to $4.5 million in revenue. Set against the US caliber's $5 million cost, the first two years mostly can't cover it; only switching to the China-caliber cost barely breaks even. This is the norm for an FDE business in its first two years: the pure-labor period, gross margin of 20% to 40%, and if you're unlucky, negative.

There are only three levers to turn a profit: reuse rate — components and platform, pressing down per-project labor hours; price tier — raising the average contract value; renewal rate — making the second year's revenue no longer need the first year's cost. Each lever raised ten percentage points, and gross margin roughly climbs from 30% to 60% — in the numbers above, the decade of ServiceNow and company climbed exactly these three levers. McGrew's line "the amount of customization per subsequent customer decreases" is precisely the tachometer of this machine: the speed at which customization decreases is the speed at which a people business turns into a software business.

Finally, a story of "betting on the right direction." At the end of 2022, ChatGPT burst onto the scene, and everyone was guessing what would become of Palantir. Sankar made a bet later quoted repeatedly: large models would rapidly commoditize, and value would gather at the two ends — one end compute chips, the other the ontology. He pressed more than half the company's engineers onto the AI platform AIP. The result: Palantir's stock price rose from the single digits when he took over as CTO all the way to around $200; US commercial revenue grew triple digits for multiple consecutive quarters. The logic of this bet is precisely the main thread that opens the whole book: the stronger and cheaper the models, the more valuable the engineering that "connects the model to reality" becomes. (Source: Appendix C)

The endpoint of the four questions of productization is a closing of the entire FDE business model: the field detects the real world for you, the platform amplifies what was detected, and finally it settles into product, generating value continuously. This is a mutually-feeding loop: the field makes the platform more solid, and the solid platform lets the field dare to take on farther work. Once this flywheel spins, you are no longer a company that "sends people to do work," but a company that "continuously converts the world's complexity into software assets." The resume of such a company has already been written for it by the gross-margin account above.
