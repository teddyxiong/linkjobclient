# Preface

Last summer, the same number flooded my social feed: 95%.

MIT's NANDA Lab released *The GenAI Divide* in 2025. The report said that over the past three years, companies worldwide have poured $30–40 billion into generative AI, and 95% of those projects produced no value worth putting in a financial statement. Friends who build models were sharing it, friends who build software were sharing it, friends who do consulting were sharing it too. The captions varied wildly, but the anxiety was the same.

What's interesting is that on that very same timeline, I saw another piece of news: on Silicon Valley job boards, a role called "Forward Deployed Engineer" (FDE) saw its number of postings grow more than sevenfold in a single year. OpenAI was hiring, Anthropic was hiring, and over a hundred startups in the YC accelerator were all hiring. One VC flatly declared: this is the hottest role in tech right now.

On one side, a 95% mortality rate for corporate AI projects. On the other, a role whose demand surged more than sevenfold in a year. Put those two headlines side by side, and the answer isn't hard to guess: models themselves are no longer hard to get — what's hard is finding the people who can shove the model into the client's real business.

The first time I really thought about this role was when I was reading up on Palantir's rise. The company was founded in 2003 and, for two decades, stubbornly did one thing the software industry thought was obstinate: they sent their best engineers to the customer site — intelligence agencies, battlefields, oil fields, factories — to sit in the client's data swamp and write code.

Wall Street didn't get Palantir for years, dismissing it as "more like a consulting firm." And then? In 2025, its market cap blew past $400 billion, and its Rule of 40 — that health metric — was so good it sent analysts scrambling to rewrite their research overnight.

Following that thread, more clues surfaced: OpenAI quietly assembled an FDE team in 2024, and in May 2026 it went so far as to team up with 19 top-tier capital firms to launch a "deployment company" valued in the tens of billions; Anthropic, almost on the same day, was reported to have formed a matching joint venture with Blackstone. So the judgment is clear: the FDE isn't a hiring fad — it's a regime change in how software gets delivered.

## Why I'm the One Writing This Book

For the past ten years, I've been doing one thing: systematically making sense of what's happening in Silicon Valley but doesn't yet have a name back home. The FDE sits right at a familiar inflection point — Silicon Valley has already written it into its org charts, while most of the Chinese-language conversation is still stuck on "isn't this just pre-sales with a new name?"

I can say with full responsibility: it is not. But what it actually is can't be explained in a single sentence — you have to go back to the scene where it was born. How Palantir was forced into this playbook by the intelligence community's walls of secrecy; how OpenAI's engineers delivered AI in the farmlands of Iowa; how a startup called Harvey broke into the top law firms that ordinary software couldn't even get near; and the delicate relationship between the Chinese enterprise-software industry's perennial "project-based" model and the FDE.

To explain all this, I combed through every first-hand source I could find: a 51-minute post-mortem from an early Palantir executive on a podcast, the memoirs of several former employees, VCs' industry analyses, the original MIT report, dozens of companies' job postings, compensation reports, practitioners' rants on forums, and the experience talks of the first batch of practitioners in China. This book is the sediment of those materials — and it is also my judgment.

## How to Read This Book

Chapter 1 lays out the full backstory of this role: where it came from, what it is, and why now. Chapters 2 through 7 walk through the complete journey of one real delivery — finding the right problem, winning the client, getting the system actually used, defending the renewal, growing the business, and making success repeatable.

Chapter 8 goes back to the source and fully revisits several of the most brilliant on-the-ground cases. At the end of the book, we'll talk about the boundaries of this profession; the appendix holds a ready-to-use checklist of metrics, a list of people, and the sources for every data point and case in the book.

You're probably one of three people: 1) an entrepreneur wanting to dive into enterprise AI — you'll see a playbook better suited to this era; 2) an engineer looking to pivot into or already doing FDE work — you'll see the full picture of the role and its ceiling; or 3) you just want to understand how AI can actually land inside enterprises — this is the trillion-dollar question of the moment, and this book is aimed straight at it.

One last thing. While writing this book, I kept coming back to a plain truth: the complicated stuff can't be solved from behind a screen — someone always has to show up. The FDE is just the act of turning that plain truth into a profession.

May this book help you move AI out of the demo room and into the real world.

Fan Bing
