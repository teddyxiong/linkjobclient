# No Followers, No Resources: How Does an Ordinary Person Doing FDE Find Their First Customers?

**Source:** [@xiaomanhedy (Hedy Zhang)](https://x.com/xiaomanhedy/status/2085237908227682329)

Recently, the term FDE has been getting hotter.

But most content right now discusses: what kind of role is FDE? What capabilities does it require? How do you get hired as an FDE at a big company?

Very few people answer another, more practical question:

**If you don't want to join a big company, but want to do FDE in an individual capacity — with no followers, no industry influence, and no big-company background — where exactly do your first customers come from?**

Especially for people over 35 who don't want to go back into the big-company system to find a job, the real difficulty may not be learning AI.

You already know how to use quite a few AI tools and can even build automated workflows. But who's willing to hand their real business to you?

What this article wants to solve is exactly this problem.

Here, "an ordinary person doing FDE" doesn't mean applying for an engineering job; it means borrowing the FDE way of working: entering real business scenarios, finding problems, and then using AI and automation tools to help the client deliver results.

**Having AI capability is only the starting point. To find customers, you also need to proactively get close to real business.**

Below are 7 methods you can start trying.

## 1. Join or Organize Local Offline OPC Events

Nowadays many cities have OPC — that is, "One-Person Company" — related offline events.

Participants are usually freelancers, one-person-company founders, and personal-studio owners. They're interested in AI and have plenty of real problems, but most don't know how to apply AI to their business.

You can start by attending events others organize.

**Don't rush to introduce how many tools you know, and don't pitch your services right away.** First understand what the other person does, where their customers come from, and which tasks take the most time each day.

When you spot a specific problem, you can offer:

> "This step could be simplified with AI. After the event, I can help you sort out your current workflow for free."

If there's no suitable local event, you can organize one yourself.

Invite 8 to 15 local entrepreneurs, find a café, a startup community, or a co-working space, and exchange ideas around a specific topic.

For example:

- How AI can reduce repetitive work in a one-person company;
- How to improve efficiency with AI without adding staff.

During the event, arrange a simple workflow demo; afterward, collect participants' contact info and follow up within 24 hours.

Your first event may not bring customers right away. But as you keep attending or organizing such events, **people will gradually associate you with "AI implementation."**

## 2. Visit Local Merchants Directly

If you need to reach customers right now, the most direct way is to walk into local merchants.

You can start with service industries like beauty, rehabilitation, housekeeping, renovation, pets, and education/training — ideally pick a field you're relatively familiar with first.

Before visiting, get a simple understanding of how the shop receives customers, handles appointments, publishes content, and does after-sales.

When you're there, don't directly ask:

> "Do you need AI?"

Most owners don't know whether they need it, and don't have time for abstract AI concepts.

You can ask in a different way:

> "Do you have anything you need to do repeatedly every day that's especially time-consuming?"

It might be replying to customer questions, organizing appointment information, writing social-media posts, sorting customer feedback, or doing after-sales follow-ups.

**What you're looking for isn't a company that "wants to buy AI," but a company that's currently troubled by a specific problem.**

Direct visits are tiring and easily rejected. **But they let you hear real needs fastest and learn what customers are willing to pay for.**

## 3. Find Potential Customers via Xiaohongshu, Douyin, and Channels

In China, you can find target customers through Xiaohongshu, Douyin, Video Channels (视频号), and official accounts, then communicate via direct message or WeChat.

For example, if you want to serve the local beauty industry, search for beauty salons, skincare-management institutions, and related owners in your city.

First see what content they post, what their main business is, and what customer questions appear in the comments, then send a targeted message:

> "Hi, I noticed you often need to reply to the same kinds of customer questions. I've been researching how to use AI to reduce this kind of repetitive work. If you're open to it, I can help you find one step that's worth optimizing first — for free."

**The purpose of the first message isn't to close a deal — it's to open a conversation.**

Compared with mass-sending hundreds of ads, **carefully researching and contacting 5 potential customers a day** is more likely to get a response.

## 4. First Do a Free AI Workflow Diagnosis for an Acquaintance

In everyone's WeChat, there are more or less some people who are working, running a business, or managing a personal venture.

You can first invite one or two acquaintances and do a free AI workflow diagnosis for them, focusing on three questions:

- What things repeat every day or every week?
- Which task is the most time-consuming and error-prone?
- Which step has relatively fixed procedures?

Then pick one scenario and judge whether it can be handed to AI, or whether manual work can be reduced through automation.

For example: turning meeting notes into to-dos, generating draft replies from customer inquiries, or generating weekly reports and follow-up reminders from a fixed template.

**Don't try to transform the other person's entire business the first time.**

**As long as you solve one specific problem and let them see that AI truly reduces the workload, there's a chance to move on to discussing fuller paid cooperation.**

## 5. Find Partners Who Have the Same Target Customers

You don't have to find all customers on your own.

Finance & tax advisors, corporate training institutions, software service providers, marketing companies, business consultants, and industry community owners usually already have a batch of enterprise clients.

These clients often ask them: how should a company use AI? Which tasks can be automated?

But these service providers may not be able to do the AI diagnosis and implementation themselves.

You can tell them:

> "If your clients run into AI-tool or workflow problems, I can help assess first; for the ones worth solving, we can design a cooperation model together."

Besides referrals, you can also co-host events.

For example, co-organize with a finance & tax advisor an event on "how AI reduces financial repetitive work in a one-person company," or share with a content service provider "how to build a content-production workflow with AI."

**They add new service value for their clients, and you gain access to their customer base.**

## 6. Cooperate With OPC Startup Communities or Co-Working Spaces

OPC startup communities and co-working spaces gather a large number of one-person companies, freelancers, and micro business owners.

You can contact the community manager and propose a simple cooperation:

Every week, hold a fixed one-hour free "AI workflow clinic" in the community. Members who run into problems with AI tools, repetitive work, or automation can come and talk.

The community gains an added member service; members get concrete help; and you get to be in long-term contact with real business needs.

Start with a four-week trial, solving one or two small problems each week. If someone develops a fuller need, schedule time for deeper discussion.

Offline events help you get to know a group of people in a concentrated way, while **a fixed workflow clinic lets you enter a startup community long-term and gradually become the person people think of when they hit AI problems.**

## 7. Keep Building in Public and Learning in Public

At the beginning, you may not have customer case studies yet, but that doesn't mean you have nothing to share.

You can go public with your learning and practice process:

- What workflow you studied today;
- What problem you tried to solve;
- What failed along the way, and what adjustments you made.

This is Learn in Public: learning openly.

When you start doing diagnoses for friends, attending OPC events, or reaching local merchants, you can also share the real practice process — this is Build in Public.

If you have no formal client, share your own experiments; if you only have a small result, just talk about that small result.

**Don't fabricate cases, and don't package a single attempt as a mature methodology.**

Continuously sharing this progress **lets others know you're seriously studying AI implementation and willing to solve concrete problems.** When they hit a similar problem, they'll be more likely to think of you.

## First Pick One Method and Stick With It for 7 Days

The easiest trap for an ordinary person doing FDE is thinking they still need to learn a few more tools, build another website, and accumulate more followers before they're qualified to reach customers.

But real FDE capability has to be trained in real business.

**You don't need to execute all 7 methods at once.**

First pick one and stick with it for 7 days: attend an OPC event, visit a few local merchants, contact a few potential customers, or complete one free AI workflow diagnosis for an acquaintance.

First find a real person, an ongoing problem, and one specific step that AI can improve.

**Your first customers are often hidden in these actions that are close enough to real business.**
